When people hear the words "office politics," they usually picture something unpleasant: factions, flattering the boss, stealing credit, talking behind someone's back, or playing a game so calculated that you have to be clever just to avoid losing.

I used to think about it that way too. The natural response was to stay away from all of it: I would simply do my job well, refuse to "play politics," and let the results speak for themselves.

But the longer I work, the more I see that office politics does not have to be a dirty game. In its healthiest form, it is simply **how people build trust, connection, and influence inside the same organization**. Put even more simply, it is how we treat each other well enough to make the place where we spend eight hours a day better for everyone.

## A company is more than an org chart

On paper, a company runs on departments, job titles, processes, and reporting lines. In practice, work often moves through another, less visible layer: relationships.

When you urgently need someone from another team to help investigate a problem, whom do you approach? When you have an idea that is not fully formed, whom do you trust enough to share it with first? When a project goes wrong, who is willing to sit with you for another thirty minutes and look for the cause instead of saying, "That's not my job"?

The answer is rarely found in the org chart alone. It lives in all the times two people have helped each other, shared lunch, or had an honest enough conversation to understand the pressure the other person is carrying.

Expertise helps you do your own job well. Good relationships help many people do their jobs well together. A company needs both.

## Big relationships start with small things

Building connections at work can sound like a serious project: go networking, learn to talk to executives, show up in the right places, and meet the right people. But most of the work relationships I value did not begin that way.

They might begin when you offer to pick up a coffee for a colleague while getting your own. With a game together during lunch. By sitting next to a new teammate during their first meal at the company. Or by asking, "How are things?" and actually stopping to hear the answer.

These moments are too small to appear in anyone's weekly report. Yet each one teaches us a little more about the person working beside us. They stop being "the person from QA," "that DevOps guy," or a name that appears only when we need an approval. They become a person with a story, interests, pressures, and bad days just like ours.

That familiarity does not replace competence, but it removes a great deal of friction from collaboration. It becomes easier to ask for help, easier to tell the truth, and easier to give each other grace when something goes wrong. A dry text message is less likely to feel hostile when the two people reading it have laughed through a game together.

## Influence is the outcome, not the goal

When your web of connections becomes broad and strong enough, something interesting happens: you begin to have influence, even if your title has not changed.

People ask for your opinion because they know you will listen. They share problems earlier because they trust that you will not use those problems to assign blame. When you introduce two people from different teams, work that was stuck starts moving again. When you suggest a change, others are willing to give it a chance because, through many small interactions, they have come to understand how you think and trust what you intend.

To me, that is a positive form of office politics. Influence does not come from being the loudest person in the room. It comes from enough people believing that working with you is a good experience. You do not always need authority to move things forward; sometimes you need trust in enough of the right places.

The order matters, though. If you buy someone coffee only because you expect to ask for a favor later, people can usually feel it. If you connect only with senior people or those who are useful to you, you have returned to the same kind of politics that made the idea feel ugly in the first place.

Kindness should not be a tactic. Treat people well because that is the environment you want to work in too. Any influence that follows is the compound interest of trust, not a debt others owe you for a few cups of coffee.

## Start with the circle closest to you

You do not need to become the most outgoing person in the company, and you do not have to force yourself into every social event. Healthy office politics does not require knowing everyone. It only asks that you stop seeing the people around you as job titles that exchange tasks.

This week, you can begin with something very small: invite a colleague for coffee, join a game at lunch, thank the person who quietly helped the project, or start a conversation with someone you have known only through a few chat messages.

There may be no immediate benefit afterward. That is exactly how it should be.

Over time, though, small conversations become familiarity. Familiarity becomes trust. Trust makes collaboration easier. Then one day you realize that you can connect people, untangle problems, and help a good idea travel further - not because you learned to play a political game brilliantly, but because you treated people well for long enough.

Maybe office politics is not as frightening as we imagine. Maybe, at its best, it is simply the art of making work feel more human - beginning with a cup of coffee.
